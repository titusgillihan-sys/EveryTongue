How it works

Three plain-English ideas explain the whole tool.

1. In Mandarin, pitch is part of the word

In English, you can say a word high, low, rising, or flat, and it's still the same word — you've just changed your tone of voice. Mandarin doesn't work that way: the pitch pattern of a syllable is part of the word. The classic example:

妈mā1st — flatmother
麻má2nd — risinghemp
马mǎ3rd — dippinghorse
骂mà4th — fallingto scold

Same sound, four different pitches, four unrelated words.

2. A melody locks in a pitch, whether or not it fits

A song's tune assigns every syllable a fixed note. When a hymn is translated into Mandarin but kept on its original Western melody, nobody adjusted the tune to fit the new words' tones — so the melody can push a syllable's pitch the wrong direction. Each of the five Mandarin tones implies where the pitch should go next relative to the previous note:

ToneShapeMelody should move
1stflat / highsame
2ndrisingup
3rddipping / lowdown
4thfallingdown
neutral—flexible, always OK

When a melody note moves the wrong way for a syllable's tone, that's a mismatch — and depending on the word, it can either blur into a different real word, or just stop sounding like a real word at all.

3. The check itself is simple and see-through — not AI, not a black box

Every syllable's tone comes from a small pinyin/tone dictionary (formatted the way the public CC-CEDICT dataset records it). The melody is a plain list of "up / down / same" steps. Comparing the two is one lookup and one equality check — the same logic runs identically on every page of this site, so nothing is hidden or hand-waved.

AI only enters at the very last step: once a mismatch is found, it's used to suggest 2–3 alternate Mandarin words that keep the original meaning while fitting the tone the melody actually needs.
